There's a strange kind of sadness spreading through engineering circles right now, and nobody's quite named it yet. It's not burnout exactly. It's not just fear of layoffs either, though that's tangled up in it somewhere. It feels closer to grief. The quiet, confusing kind you feel when something you built your whole identity around stops mattering the way it used to.

For most of us, the job was never really "write code." That was just the visible output, the thing you could point to and say "I made that." The real job, the part that made a 2am debugging session feel less like punishment and more like a story you'd tell later, was a private loop nobody actually paid you for directly:

- **Comprehend** the problem until you can finally see its shape, even in the dark.
- **Strategize** a solution, try a few in your head, throw half of them out, argue with yourself about the rest.
- **Translate** all that thinking into actual code, one line at a time.
- **Debug**, that special kind of pain when a stack trace lies to you, followed by a trip through decade-old Stack Overflow answers written by people who probably don't code for a living anymore.
- **Finish.** That specific, physical relief when something that looked impossible an hour, a day, or a week ago is finally, actually done.

That loop was the craft. You never got paid extra for it, but it's the reason you stayed at your desk past 6pm without even noticing the time.

## The lifecycle, automated end to end

Here's the uncomfortable part. It's not that AI writes code now. Most of us made peace with smart autocomplete a while back. The uncomfortable part is that the whole loop is getting swallowed, one piece at a time. There's a plugin or a skill or an agent for comprehension. Another for strategy. Another for implementation. Another for the debug-and-patch cycle. And more often now, someone chains all of it together so the entire lifecycle runs while a human mostly just clicks approve on the final diff. In some dev environments, even that click is going away: the review itself is done by another AI, and the whole thing merges on its own within a set of guardrails.

That's not a workflow upgrade anymore. That's removing the exact thing that made the workflow worth doing in the first place. You can automate a task away and lose nothing but time. You can't automate a craft away without also erasing the reason ten years of practicing it shaped the way you think: the judgment, the taste, the instincts you can't fully explain to a junior engineer because they live somewhere below language.

## Enter: the meat proxy

There's a phrase going around developer circles this month that's landed harder than most tech slang usually does. A developer named Niklas Gruhn coined "meat proxy" to describe someone who forwards AI generated answers to a colleague without reading, understanding, or checking them first: a human relay adding nothing but delay between a chatbot and the person who could've just asked it themselves. [Cybernews has a solid writeup](https://cybernews.com/ai-news/meat-proxies-insecure-people/) on how fast the term spread.

Gruhn's original target was narrower than the crisis a lot of engineers are living through right now. He was calling out one specific bad habit, not an entire profession. But the phrase clearly hit a nerve for a bigger reason. If a pipeline can understand the ticket, plan the fix, write the code, and respond to your review comments on its own, the honest question left over is: what part of this loop actually still needs me to be a person, instead of a relay between a ticket queue and an agent? That's the real sting. Not that the term was written about you specifically, but that it describes a role a lot of engineers can feel themselves sliding into.

And once you start asking that question seriously, a harder one shows up right behind it. Why spend another ten years sharpening a craft that no longer gives you the feeling that made it worth sharpening?

## Exhibit A: The retired Geese Farmer

Turns out plenty of senior, accomplished engineers have already answered that question with their feet, or at least that's the story that goes around. LinkedIn has quietly turned into a graveyard of "22 years at [Big Tech], now I raise geese" posts. Half genuine pastoral pivot, half elaborate coping mechanism, entirely relatable. [Here's one making the rounds](https://lnkd.in/p/dbfJFK4Y).

The most famous version of that story is Feng Yuan, a Principal Software Development Engineer who spent 22 years at Microsoft. A viral post framed it as "resigned after 22 years just to become a goose farmer," which is the version that made the internet swoon. The real story is a little less romantic: [Yuan was let go during Microsoft's 2023 layoffs](https://80.lv/articles/former-microsoft-veteran-who-became-goose-farmer-sold-his-animals-is-now-bonsai-master), and he later clarified it was tied to a performance review, not a graceful exit. He'd already been raising geese for about a year before that happened, just as a hobby, and simply leaned into it once the job was gone. The story still resonated with enough tech workers to go properly viral, maybe because the ending is the same either way.

## Exhibit B: the CEO who launched Goat-as-a-Service

Not everyone waits for a layoff to make the leap. [Farrukh Malik](https://www.linkedin.com/in/farrukhmalikk/), a tech entrepreneur in Islamabad, is still running his IT company. He's just also running Kallu Farms on the side, a premium goat farm with its own branded stall and gift packs. Call it Goat-as-a-Service: same CEO energy, entirely different infrastructure.

Somewhere between these two stories is a pretty honest answer to the question this whole post keeps circling. If the joy of building software is thinning out, maybe you don't need to burn the career down to feel something again. Maybe you just build something else alongside it.

Whether or not the geese (or the goats) are actually more fulfilling than a well-refactored service, there's something telling about why these posts get shared with a knowing laugh instead of confusion. Nobody's confused about the joke anymore.

## So — do we lose hope, or do we buy land?

Maybe the honest answer is that the craft isn't dying so much as moving up a level — from writing the code to knowing which code is worth writing, from debugging syntax to debugging systems, from meat proxy to the thing standing *between* the agents and the outcome that actually matters. Maybe that's a smaller, stranger joy than the old one, and maybe it's one we haven't fully learned to feel yet because we're still grieving the version we're used to.

Or maybe the geese know something we don't.

So, fellow engineers: do we hold the line and go looking for the next version of the joy — or do we start pricing out acreage on the outskirts of the city, just in case?
